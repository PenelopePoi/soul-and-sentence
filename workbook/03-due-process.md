# Section 3: Due Process — The 5th & 14th Amendments

## Procedural Due Process

Before the government can deprive you of life, liberty, or property, it must provide:

- **Notice** — You must be told what you are accused of
- **Hearing** — You must have an opportunity to be heard
- **Neutral decision-maker** — The person deciding must be impartial

In prison, due process applies to: disciplinary proceedings resulting in solitary, loss of gain time, transfer to higher security, and parole revocation hearings.

## Substantive Due Process

The government cannot: punish you for exercising a constitutional right (retaliation), impose punishments that "shock the conscience," deny access to courts, or prevent religious practice without compelling security reason.

## Key Cases

- **Wolff v. McDonnell (1974)** — Minimum due process for prison disciplinary hearings: 24 hours advance written notice, opportunity to call witnesses, written statement of reasons.
- **Sandin v. Conner (1995)** — Due process protections triggered only when punishment imposes "atypical and significant hardship."
- **Morrissey v. Brewer (1972)** — Parolees entitled to due process before revocation.

## What to Do If Violated

1. Document everything immediately
2. File a formal grievance
3. Appeal disciplinary decisions
4. If administrative remedies exhausted, file a Section 1983 lawsuit
5. Contact legal aid organizations

## Journal Prompts

1. Have you been disciplined without proper notice or fair hearing? Describe what happened.
2. What does "fairness" mean to you personally?
3. Write a letter explaining why due process matters — even for people who have been convicted.
