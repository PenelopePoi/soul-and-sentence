# Section 2: Search & Seizure — The 4th Amendment in Practice

## What the 4th Amendment Says

"The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause."

## How It Applies in Jail or Prison

- **Cell searches:** Under Hudson v. Palmer (1984), officers can search your cell at any time. However, they cannot destroy property out of harassment.
- **Body searches:** Strip searches require reasonable suspicion. Invasive body cavity searches must be conducted by medical professionals.
- **Pat-downs:** Routine pat-downs are generally allowed without suspicion.
- **Legal mail:** May only be opened in your presence to check for contraband. Contents may not be read.
- **Cross-gender searches:** Under PREA, cross-gender strip searches are heavily restricted except in emergencies.

## Key Cases

- **Bell v. Wolfish (1979)** — Visual body cavity inspections after contact visits do not violate the 4th Amendment when part of a reasonable security protocol.
- **Florence v. Board of Chosen Freeholders (2012)** — Jails may strip-search all individuals entering general population without requiring reasonable suspicion.

## How to Document a Search Violation

1. Write down the date, time, and location immediately
2. Note names or badge numbers of all officers involved
3. Describe exactly what happened, in order
4. Note any witnesses
5. Write down any injuries, damage to property, or threats made
6. File a grievance within the required time frame
7. Keep a copy of everything
