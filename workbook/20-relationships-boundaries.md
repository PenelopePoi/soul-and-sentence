# Section 20: Relationships & Boundaries

Healthy relationships are built on respect, honesty, and boundaries.

## Why Relationships Matter

Human beings are wired for connection. We need other people — not just for practical survival, but for emotional well-being. Loneliness is as damaging to your health as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. Connection is not optional. It is a biological need.

But not all connection is healthy. Some relationships give you life. Others drain it. Some relationships push you toward who you want to become. Others pull you back toward who you are trying to leave behind.

Learning to tell the difference — and having the skills to build healthy relationships while letting go of destructive ones — is one of the most important things you can do, both in here and out there.

## Types of Boundaries

A boundary is a line that defines where you end and another person begins. Boundaries are not walls — walls keep everyone out. Boundaries are doors — you decide who comes in, how far, and under what conditions.

### Physical Boundaries

These are about your body and your space. You have the right to:
- Decide who touches you and how.
- Have personal space respected.
- Say no to physical contact, even if it is "just a handshake" or "just a hug."
- Protect your body from harm.

In incarceration, physical boundaries are constantly violated by the system itself. That does not mean you abandon the concept. It means you exercise it wherever you can and hold onto the principle for when you are free.

### Emotional Boundaries

These are about your feelings, energy, and mental health. You have the right to:
- Choose not to absorb other people's emotions and problems.
- Decline conversations that damage your mental state.
- Not be responsible for other people's feelings.
- Keep certain thoughts and feelings private.
- Refuse to engage with manipulation, guilt trips, or emotional blackmail.

### Time Boundaries

These are about how you spend your time and attention. You have the right to:
- Decide how you spend your free time.
- End conversations that are not productive or healthy.
- Prioritize activities that support your goals and well-being.
- Not be at anyone's beck and call.

### Material Boundaries

These are about your possessions and resources. You have the right to:
- Decide what you share and what you keep.
- Say no to requests for your commissary, your books, your supplies.
- Not lend things you cannot afford to lose.
- Not be guilted into giving away what you need.

In incarceration, material boundaries are especially important. A culture of sharing and reciprocity can be positive, but it can also be exploitative. Know the difference.

## Signs of Unhealthy Relationships

Not every unhealthy relationship involves physical violence. Many are subtler. Here are the warning signs:

### Control
- The other person tries to dictate who you talk to, what you do, or how you think.
- They monitor your activities, your mail, your phone calls.
- They make decisions for you without your input.
- They isolate you from other relationships.

### Manipulation
- They twist your words or make you doubt your own perception (gaslighting).
- They guilt you into doing things you do not want to do.
- They use favors as leverage: "After everything I have done for you..."
- They play victim when confronted about their behavior.

### Disrespect
- They belittle you, mock you, or call you names — even "jokingly."
- They dismiss your feelings: "You are too sensitive" or "You are overreacting."
- They share your private information with others.
- They consistently break promises.

### Imbalance
- You give far more than you receive.
- The relationship only works when you do what they want.
- Your needs are treated as less important than theirs.
- You feel exhausted, drained, or smaller after spending time with them.

### Threats
- They threaten you — physically, socially, or legally.
- They threaten self-harm to keep you from leaving.
- They use your vulnerabilities against you.
- They make you afraid of the consequences of standing up for yourself.

**Check yourself:** Look at this list and think about your current relationships — inside and outside. Do any of these patterns apply? Which side of the pattern are you on?

## Signs of Healthy Relationships

Healthy relationships feel different in your body. You feel safe, not on guard. You feel respected, not managed. You feel free, not trapped.

### Mutual Respect
- Both people treat each other as equals.
- Disagreements happen without personal attacks.
- Both people's feelings and opinions are valued.

### Honesty
- Both people can be truthful without fear of punishment.
- There are no hidden agendas or games.
- When someone makes a mistake, they own it.

### Trust
- Both people follow through on commitments.
- Neither person feels the need to monitor or control the other.
- Vulnerability is met with care, not exploitation.

### Independence
- Both people maintain their own identity, interests, and goals.
- Time apart is not a threat.
- Neither person needs the other to complete them.

### Support
- Both people encourage each other's growth.
- They celebrate each other's wins without jealousy.
- They show up during hard times without keeping score.

### Accountability
- Both people take responsibility for their actions.
- They apologize genuinely and change the behavior, not just the words.
- They accept feedback without becoming defensive.

## The Boundary-Setting Formula

Setting a boundary can feel confrontational, especially if you have never done it before or if you come from an environment where speaking up was dangerous. Here is a simple formula:

**"When you [specific behavior], I feel [your feeling]. I need [what you need]. If [the behavior continues], I will [your action]."**

Examples:

- "When you raise your voice at me, I feel disrespected. I need you to speak to me calmly. If you continue yelling, I will end this conversation."

- "When you ask to borrow my commissary every week, I feel taken advantage of. I need you to stop asking. If you keep asking, I will not be able to continue this friendship."

- "When you talk negatively about my goals, I feel unsupported. I need you to either support me or keep your opinion to yourself. If you keep putting me down, I will spend less time with you."

### Important Notes About Boundary Setting

**You do not have to justify your boundaries.** "No" is a complete sentence. You can explain if you want to, but you are not required to.

**Boundaries are about your behavior, not theirs.** You cannot control what other people do. You can only control what you do in response. A boundary states what you will do if the behavior continues, not what they must do.

**Some people will not respect your boundaries.** That tells you everything you need to know about the relationship. A person who punishes you for setting a boundary is showing you exactly why the boundary was necessary.

**Boundaries feel uncomfortable at first.** Especially if you are used to people-pleasing, going along to get along, or keeping the peace at your own expense. The discomfort does not mean you are doing something wrong. It means you are doing something new.

## Relationships and Reentry

When you get out, your relationships will be tested. Some things to prepare for:

- **People from your past will reappear.** Not all of them have your best interests at heart. Have a plan for how you will handle invitations back into old patterns.
- **Family dynamics may have shifted.** People moved on while you were gone. Children grew up. Partners found their own rhythms. Reentering takes patience, communication, and the willingness to rebuild rather than demand.
- **You have changed.** Or you are changing. And not everyone will welcome the new version of you. Some people preferred the old you because the old you was easier to control, predict, or use.
- **New relationships need a strong foundation.** Take your time. Watch how people treat others, not just you. Pay attention to actions, not just words.

## Journal Prompts

1. Think about the most important relationship in your life right now. Is it healthy? Using the signs listed in this section, honestly evaluate it. Where does it fall short, and what boundary might improve it?

2. What boundary do you have the hardest time setting, and why? Is it physical, emotional, time, or material? Where did you learn that setting that boundary was not okay?

3. Write about a relationship you need to let go of — or at least change significantly. What is it costing you to keep it the way it is? What would your life look like if you set the boundary you have been avoiding?
