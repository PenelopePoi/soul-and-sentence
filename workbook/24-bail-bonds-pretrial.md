# Section 24: Bail, Bonds & Pretrial Procedures

Types of release — ROR, cash bail, surety bond, property bond, pretrial services.

## Why You Need to Understand This

Whether you are currently awaiting trial, helping a family member, or just want to understand the system you are in — knowing how bail and pretrial release works gives you power. The pretrial phase is one of the most consequential periods in a criminal case, and most people go through it without understanding what is happening or what their options are.

People who do not make bail sit in jail for weeks, months, sometimes years before their case is resolved. Studies show that people held pretrial are more likely to plead guilty — not because they are guilty, but because they are desperate to get out. They lose jobs, housing, custody, and stability while sitting in a cell waiting for a trial they are constitutionally promised.

Understanding the system does not guarantee a different outcome. But it puts you in a position to advocate for yourself or help someone you love.

## Types of Pretrial Release

When you are arrested and charged with a crime, the court must decide whether to release you before trial and under what conditions. There are several types of release:

### Release on Own Recognizance (ROR)

This means you are released without paying any money. You simply sign a promise to appear at all court dates. The court is trusting that you will come back.

**Who gets ROR:**
- People charged with minor, nonviolent offenses
- People with no criminal history or minimal history
- People with strong community ties (stable job, family, long-term residence)
- People who are not considered a flight risk or danger to the community

**What you need to know:**
- ROR is free, but it comes with conditions. You must appear at every court date. Failing to appear can result in an arrest warrant and additional charges.
- If you are denied ROR, your attorney can argue for it at the bail hearing.

### Cash Bail

The court sets a dollar amount that you must pay to be released. The money is held by the court as a guarantee that you will appear. If you show up to all court dates, the money is returned to you at the end of the case (minus any court fees). If you fail to appear, you lose the money and face additional charges.

**How it works:**
- The judge sets the bail amount based on the charges, your criminal history, flight risk, and danger to the community.
- You (or someone on your behalf) pays the full amount to the court.
- You are released.
- When the case is resolved — whether by plea, dismissal, or trial — the bail money is returned.

**The problem with cash bail:**
Cash bail disproportionately punishes poverty. If bail is set at $5,000, a wealthy person goes home and a poor person stays in jail — even if they are charged with the same crime. This is one of the most actively debated issues in criminal justice reform.

### Surety Bond (Bail Bondsman)

If you cannot afford the full cash bail, you can use a bail bondsman. Here is how it works:

- The bondsman charges you a fee — typically 10% of the bail amount. This fee is nonrefundable. It is the bondsman's payment for taking the risk.
- The bondsman posts the full bail amount with the court on your behalf.
- You are released.
- If you appear at all court dates, the bondsman gets their money back from the court. You do not get your 10% back — that is their profit.
- If you fail to appear, the bondsman loses their money and will come looking for you (this is where bounty hunters come in). You also face additional charges.

**Example:** Bail is set at $10,000. You pay a bondsman $1,000 (10%). The bondsman posts $10,000 with the court. You are released. You never get your $1,000 back regardless of the outcome.

**What to watch for:**
- Some bondsmen charge additional fees (financing charges, administrative fees). Read everything before you sign.
- Some bondsmen require collateral (car title, property, jewelry). If you fail to appear, they can seize the collateral.
- The 10% fee is standard but can vary by state.

### Property Bond

Instead of cash, you or a family member puts up property (usually real estate) as collateral. The property must have equity equal to or greater than the bail amount.

**How it works:**
- The court places a lien on the property.
- If you appear at all court dates, the lien is removed when the case is resolved.
- If you fail to appear, the court can foreclose on the property.

**Important:** This is a serious commitment. If you skip court, your family member could lose their home. Do not take this lightly.

### Pretrial Services / Supervised Release

Some jurisdictions have pretrial services programs that provide an alternative to cash bail. Instead of paying money, you are released under supervision with conditions.

**Common conditions include:**
- Regular check-ins with a pretrial officer (in person or by phone)
- Drug testing
- GPS/ankle monitoring
- Curfew
- Travel restrictions
- No contact with victims or co-defendants
- Mental health or substance abuse treatment

**Who qualifies:**
Eligibility varies by jurisdiction. Pretrial services programs use risk assessment tools to determine whether you are likely to appear in court and whether you pose a safety risk.

**Benefits:** You do not have to pay bail. You receive some level of support and supervision.

**Risks:** Violating any condition can result in immediate re-arrest and revocation of release.

### Federal Pretrial Release

Federal cases work differently from state cases. In federal court:
- There is no bail bondsman system. You either pay the full amount or you do not.
- The judge considers four factors: risk of flight, danger to the community, nature of the offense, and weight of the evidence.
- Many federal defendants are detained pretrial, especially for drug offenses, firearms offenses, and violent crimes.
- If released, conditions are supervised by U.S. Pretrial Services.

## Your Rights at a Bail Hearing

You have rights at your bail hearing, and knowing them can make a difference:

### Right to a Hearing

You are entitled to a bail hearing. The court cannot hold you indefinitely without one (though delays happen). In most jurisdictions, a bail hearing must occur within 24-48 hours of arrest.

### Right to an Attorney

You have the right to have an attorney present at your bail hearing. If you do not have one, a public defender should be assigned. If no attorney is present and bail is set, your attorney can request a new hearing later.

### Right to Present Evidence

You or your attorney can present evidence in your favor:
- Proof of employment
- Community ties (family, church, organizations)
- Length of residency
- Evidence of prior compliance (previous cases where you appeared as required)
- Character witnesses

### Right to Challenge the Bail Amount

If bail is set too high, your attorney can argue for a reduction. The Eighth Amendment prohibits "excessive bail." While courts have wide discretion, the bail amount must be related to ensuring your appearance and community safety — not used as punishment.

### What to Prepare for Your Bail Hearing

If possible, have the following ready:
- Proof of employment or job offer
- Proof of address (lease, utility bill, letter from family member)
- Letters of support from family, employers, clergy, or community members
- Information about your ties to the community
- A proposed release plan (where you will live, how you will support yourself, who will supervise you)

## What Happens If You Cannot Make Bail

If you cannot afford bail and do not qualify for ROR or pretrial services, you will be held in jail until your case is resolved. This can take weeks, months, or even years.

**What you can do:**
- Ask your attorney to file a motion to reduce bail. Circumstances change — if you lose your job while sitting in jail, that is a reason to request a reduction.
- Contact a bail fund. Several organizations pay bail for people who cannot afford it (see Resources below).
- Explore pretrial diversion programs. Depending on your charge, you may qualify for a diversion program that resolves the case without trial.
- Stay informed about your case. Ask your attorney about every hearing, every motion, every development. Do not let your case go silent.

## Understanding Conditions of Release

Whether you are released on ROR, bond, or pretrial services, your release will come with conditions. Violating those conditions can result in:
- Revocation of release (you go back to jail)
- Additional criminal charges
- Forfeiture of bail money
- Negative impact on your case outcome

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Missing a court date (even accidentally). Put every date on a calendar. Set reminders. Have someone else remind you. There is no acceptable excuse for missing court.
- Failing a drug test. If drug testing is a condition, take it seriously. A failed test can put you right back in a cell.
- Contacting someone you are ordered to stay away from. Even a "friendly" text or call through a third party counts as a violation.
- Traveling outside the allowed area without permission.
- Getting arrested for a new offense.

## Key Terms

- **Bail:** Money or property deposited with the court to ensure you appear at future court dates.
- **Bond:** A written promise to pay a specified amount if you fail to appear. Often used interchangeably with bail but technically refers to the agreement, not the money.
- **ROR (Release on Own Recognizance):** Release without paying bail, based on your promise to appear.
- **Surety:** A third party (bail bondsman) who guarantees your appearance by posting bail on your behalf.
- **Collateral:** Property or assets pledged to secure a bond.
- **Forfeiture:** Loss of bail money when a defendant fails to appear.
- **FTA (Failure to Appear):** Missing a scheduled court date, which can result in a warrant and additional charges.
- **Lien:** A legal claim on property, used in property bonds.
- **Pretrial Detention:** Being held in jail before trial because bail was denied or cannot be paid.
