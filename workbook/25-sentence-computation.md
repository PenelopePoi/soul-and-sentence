# Section 25: Sentence Computation & Gain Time

Florida 85% rule, basic gain time, incentive gain time, meritorious gain time.

## Why This Matters

Your release date is not just a number on a piece of paper. It is a calculation — and that calculation involves rules, credits, and formulas that you need to understand. Errors in sentence computation happen more often than people think, and they can mean the difference between months or even years behind bars.

Understanding how your sentence is calculated empowers you to:
- Verify that your release date is correct.
- Maximize the gain time available to you.
- Catch errors that could keep you locked up longer than the law requires.
- Have informed conversations with your attorney, case manager, or the Department of Corrections.

This section focuses on Florida law, but the principles apply in modified form to other states. If you are in a different state, research your state's specific rules.

## The Florida 85% Rule

In 1995, Florida passed a law that fundamentally changed how sentences work. Under Florida Statute 944.275, any person convicted of a crime committed on or after October 1, 1995, must serve at least 85% of their sentence before being eligible for release.

### How It Works

If you are sentenced to 10 years (120 months):
- 85% of 120 months = 102 months
- You must serve at least 102 months (8 years, 6 months)
- The maximum gain time you can earn reduces the sentence by 15%

**Important:** The 85% rule applies to the total sentence imposed, not to individual charges within a sentence. If you have consecutive sentences, each one is subject to the 85% rule separately.

### Who Does the 85% Rule Apply To?

- Anyone convicted of a crime committed **on or after October 1, 1995.**
- This includes all felonies and most misdemeanors resulting in a state prison sentence.

### Who Does the 85% Rule NOT Apply To?

- People convicted of crimes committed **before October 1, 1995.** These individuals may be eligible for older, more generous gain time rules.
- People serving federal sentences (federal sentences have different rules).
- People in county jail serving misdemeanor sentences (county jail gain time rules differ).

### Before the 85% Rule (Pre-October 1, 1995)

If your offense was committed before this date, you may be eligible for significantly more gain time under the older rules. The pre-1995 system allowed inmates to earn enough gain time to serve as little as one-third of their sentence. If you believe your offense predates the 85% rule, consult with your attorney or a legal aid organization immediately — the difference can be substantial.

## Types of Gain Time

Gain time (also called "good time") is a credit applied to your sentence that moves your release date earlier. In Florida, there are several types:

### Basic Gain Time

**What it is:** An automatic credit of 10 days per month for every month of the sentence.

**How you earn it:** It is awarded automatically as long as you remain in the custody of the Department of Corrections and are not in disciplinary confinement.

**How you lose it:** Disciplinary reports (DRs) can result in the loss of basic gain time. The severity of the loss depends on the severity of the infraction:
- Minor infractions: Loss of up to 30 days of gain time
- Major infractions: Loss of up to 60 days or more
- Violent or escape-related infractions: Loss of up to 180 days

**Important:** Once basic gain time is forfeited, it cannot be restored. Protecting your gain time is one of the most practical reasons to stay out of trouble.

### Incentive Gain Time

**What it is:** Additional gain time earned through participation in approved programs, education, and work assignments.

**How you earn it:**
- **Work assignments:** Inmates who are assigned to and satisfactorily perform work can earn incentive gain time. The amount varies based on the type of assignment and performance rating.
- **Educational programs:** Completing your GED, vocational training, or college courses can earn gain time. Specific amounts vary by program.
- **Program participation:** Substance abuse programs, anger management, life skills, and other DOC-approved programs may also qualify.

**Amounts:** Incentive gain time awards in Florida can range from a few days to several months, depending on the program and the length of the sentence.

**Important:** Not all programs qualify for gain time. Ask your classification officer or counselor which programs currently award gain time credits.

### Meritorious Gain Time

**What it is:** A special award of gain time for exceptional conduct or contributions.

**How you earn it:**
- Saving a life or preventing serious injury
- Exceptional work performance over an extended period
- Significant contributions to the facility or community
- Heroic acts during emergencies

**How much:** Up to 60 days per award in Florida.

**Reality check:** Meritorious gain time is rare. It is not something you can count on in your release date calculation. Consider it a bonus if it happens, not part of your plan.

### Education Gain Time

**What it is:** Specific gain time awarded for completing educational milestones.

**Florida specifics:**
- Earning a GED: Up to 60 days of gain time
- Completing a vocational certificate: Varies, typically 30-60 days
- College courses: May qualify under certain programs

**Important:** Education gain time may have changed or been modified since the 85% rule was enacted. Confirm current rules with your counselor or the DOC website (www.dc.state.fl.us).

## How to Calculate Your Release Date

Here is a simplified framework for calculating your projected release date in Florida under the 85% rule:

### Step 1: Start with Your Total Sentence

Example: 10 years = 3,650 days

### Step 2: Calculate 85%

3,650 days x 0.85 = 3,102.5 days (round up to 3,103 days)

This is the minimum number of days you must serve.

### Step 3: Calculate Maximum Possible Gain Time

3,650 - 3,103 = 547 days

This is the most your sentence can be reduced through gain time (15% of the total).

### Step 4: Determine Actual Gain Time Earned

Add up all gain time credits you have actually earned:
- Basic gain time: ___ days
- Incentive gain time: ___ days
- Meritorious gain time: ___ days
- Education gain time: ___ days
- **Total earned:** ___ days

### Step 5: Subtract Gain Time Lost

If you have had any disciplinary actions resulting in loss of gain time:
- Total gain time forfeited: ___ days

### Step 6: Calculate Net Gain Time

Total earned - Total forfeited = Net gain time

**Important:** Your net gain time cannot exceed the 15% maximum (547 days in our example).

### Step 7: Calculate Projected Release Date

Start date + (Total sentence - Net gain time) = Projected release date

Or: Start date + 85% of sentence + any days lost to discipline = Projected release date

## Jail Credit (Time Served)

You are entitled to credit for time served in county jail before sentencing. This is sometimes called "jail credit" or "credit for time served."

**How it works:**
- Every day you spent in custody between arrest and sentencing should be credited toward your sentence.
- This includes time in county jail, holding facilities, and any other detention.
- Jail credit is applied to the start of your sentence, effectively moving your start date earlier.

**Common errors:**
- Jail credit not being applied at all.
- Incorrect start or end dates for jail credit calculation.
- Failure to credit time spent in multiple facilities.
- Not crediting time spent on GPS monitoring or house arrest before sentencing (in some cases, this qualifies).

## Common Sentence Computation Errors

Errors happen. The DOC processes thousands of calculations, and mistakes are made. Here are the most common ones:

### 1. Incorrect Jail Credit

The most frequent error. Your jail credit may be calculated incorrectly — wrong dates, missing days, or credit not applied at all.

**How to check:** Compare the jail credit shown on your sentence computation sheet with your actual arrest date and sentencing date. Count the days yourself.

### 2. Wrong Offense Date

If the DOC has an incorrect offense date, you might be placed under the wrong gain time rules. This is especially critical if your offense was near the October 1, 1995 cutoff. The wrong date could mean the difference between 85% and the more generous pre-1995 rules.

**How to check:** Compare the offense date on your sentence computation sheet with the date on your judgment and sentence (J&S) or plea agreement.

### 3. Concurrent vs. Consecutive Confusion

If you have multiple counts or cases, the sentences may run concurrently (at the same time) or consecutively (one after another). If the DOC applies them incorrectly, your release date could be off by years.

**How to check:** Read your judgment and sentence carefully. It should state whether sentences are concurrent or consecutive. Compare this with what the DOC has in its system.

### 4. Gain Time Not Credited

Completed programs or work assignments that should have earned gain time may not have been recorded. Records get lost. Paperwork falls through cracks.

**How to check:** Keep your own records. Save every certificate, every program completion document, every work evaluation. Compare your records with the DOC's gain time log.

### 5. Gain Time Over-Forfeited

Disciplinary actions may have resulted in excessive gain time loss — more than allowed for the infraction, or forfeiture from incidents that were later overturned.

**How to check:** Review each disciplinary report and the gain time forfeited for each. Compare with DOC policy on maximum forfeiture amounts per infraction level.

## What to Do If You Find an Error

### Step 1: Document Everything

Write down exactly what the error is, with specific dates and numbers. Compare the DOC's computation with your own records.

### Step 2: Request Your Sentence Computation Sheet

You have the right to a copy of your computation sheet. Submit a written request to your classification officer or the DOC records office.

### Step 3: File an Administrative Grievance

Use your facility's grievance process (see Section 8 of this workbook). Be specific. State the error, provide your evidence, and request a correction.

### Step 4: Write to the DOC Bureau of Sentence Structure

If the grievance does not resolve the issue, write directly to:

**Florida Department of Corrections**
Bureau of Sentence Structure
501 South Calhoun Street
Tallahassee, FL 32399

Include copies (not originals) of all supporting documentation.

### Step 5: Seek Legal Help

If administrative remedies fail, consider:
- Filing a pro se motion with the sentencing court requesting a correction.
- Contacting a legal aid organization.
- Writing to the Florida Innocence Project or similar organizations if the error is substantial.

### Step 6: Keep Records of Everything

Every letter you send, every grievance you file, every response you receive — keep copies. Date everything. This creates a paper trail that supports your claim.

## Consecutive vs. Concurrent Sentences Explained

This is a frequent source of confusion, so let us be clear:

**Concurrent sentences** run at the same time. If you are sentenced to 5 years on Count 1 and 3 years on Count 2 to run concurrently, you serve 5 years total (the longer sentence). The shorter sentence runs inside the longer one.

**Consecutive sentences** run one after another. If you are sentenced to 5 years on Count 1 and 3 years on Count 2 to run consecutively, you serve 8 years total.

Under the 85% rule, each consecutive sentence is calculated separately. You must serve 85% of each one.

## Key Terms

- **Sentence computation:** The mathematical calculation of your release date.
- **Gain time:** Credits that reduce your sentence.
- **Jail credit:** Time served before sentencing that is credited toward your sentence.
- **85% rule:** Florida requirement that inmates serve at least 85% of their sentence.
- **Concurrent:** Sentences that run at the same time.
- **Consecutive:** Sentences that run one after another.
- **J&S (Judgment and Sentence):** The court document that states your sentence.
- **DR (Disciplinary Report):** A formal write-up for violating institutional rules.
- **Forfeiture:** Loss of gain time due to disciplinary action.
