# Section 15: Inner Child Healing

Inside every adult is the child they once were, still carrying wounds, fears, and unmet needs.

## What Is the Inner Child?

The "inner child" is not a metaphor. It is the part of your brain and nervous system that was shaped during childhood — the part that still reacts the way it learned to react when you were five, eight, twelve years old. When something in your adult life hits a nerve that was first exposed in childhood, it is your inner child who responds.

You have probably seen this in yourself. A grown man gets told he is not good enough and suddenly feels like a helpless kid again. A grown woman gets abandoned by someone she trusted and the pain is far bigger than the situation warrants — because it is not just about today. It is about every time.

Your inner child is not something to be embarrassed about. Acknowledging that child is one of the most courageous things you can do.

## Why This Matters Behind Bars

Many people who end up incarcerated experienced childhood trauma — abuse, neglect, abandonment, instability, violence, poverty, addiction in the home. Not all, but many. And when that trauma goes unaddressed, it drives adult behavior in ways that are hard to see from the inside.

You are not broken because you had a difficult childhood. But you may be carrying survival strategies that made sense for a child in danger and no longer serve you as an adult.

A child who learned to fight to survive may become an adult who fights when fighting is no longer necessary. A child who learned to shut down emotionally may become an adult who cannot form real connections. A child who learned that love was conditional may become an adult who performs for approval or rejects love before it can reject them.

Understanding your inner child helps you see where these patterns started — and gives you the power to choose differently.

## The Five Core Wounds

Psychologists have identified five wounds that commonly develop in childhood. Most people carry more than one. As you read through them, notice which ones resonate with you.

### Wound 1: "I Am Not Enough"

This wound forms when a child repeatedly receives the message — through words, actions, or absence — that who they are is not sufficient. Maybe your parent was never satisfied with your grades, your behavior, your appearance. Maybe you were compared to a sibling. Maybe the message was never spoken but was always felt.

**How it shows up in adults:** Perfectionism. Overachieving to prove worth. Or the opposite — giving up entirely because "what is the point, I will never be enough anyway." Difficulty accepting compliments. Constant self-criticism.

### Wound 2: "I Am Not Safe"

This wound forms when a child's environment is unpredictable or dangerous. Maybe there was violence in the home. Maybe a parent's mood could shift without warning. Maybe the neighborhood was chaotic. The child learns that the world is not a safe place and that they must always be on guard.

**How it shows up in adults:** Hypervigilance — always scanning for threats. Difficulty relaxing or sleeping. Distrust of others. Need for control. Explosive reactions to perceived disrespect, because disrespect feels like a physical threat.

### Wound 3: "I Am Not Worthy"

This wound is related to the first but goes deeper. "Not enough" says "I need to do more." "Not worthy" says "I do not deserve good things at all." This wound often forms through abandonment, severe neglect, or being told explicitly that you are worthless, that you were a mistake, that you ruined someone's life.

**How it shows up in adults:** Self-sabotage — destroying good things before they can be taken away. Choosing partners, friends, and situations that confirm the belief. Difficulty imagining a good future. Substance use to numb the feeling of unworthiness.

### Wound 4: "I Am Invisible"

This wound forms when a child's emotional needs are consistently ignored. Maybe your parents were physically present but emotionally absent. Maybe you were the "easy" child who did not make trouble, so nobody checked in on you. Maybe your family had so many crises that your needs were always last.

**How it shows up in adults:** People-pleasing — trying to earn visibility through being useful. Or attention-seeking through negative behavior, because even bad attention is better than none. Difficulty asking for what you need. Feeling like you do not matter.

### Wound 5: "I Must Perform to Be Loved"

This wound forms when love is conditional — when a child learns that affection, approval, or basic safety depends on meeting expectations. Love becomes a transaction. You must earn it through behavior, achievement, silence, or compliance.

**How it shows up in adults:** Inability to rest or be still. Constant hustle and productivity tied to self-worth. Fear of being seen as lazy or useless. Resentment when effort is not recognized. Difficulty believing someone could love you just as you are, without you doing anything.

## Meet Your Inner Child: A Visualization Exercise

Find a quiet moment. Close your eyes if you feel safe doing so. Take three slow breaths.

Now imagine yourself walking down a hallway. At the end of the hallway is a room. In that room is you as a child — whatever age comes to mind first. Do not force it. Let the image come naturally.

Look at that child. Notice what they are wearing. Notice their expression. Notice their posture. Are they scared? Angry? Sad? Withdrawn? Trying to look tough?

Now imagine sitting down next to them. Not in front of them — beside them. You are not there to interrogate them. You are there to be with them.

Say to them, silently or out loud:

- "I see you."
- "What happened to you was not your fault."
- "You did the best you could with what you had."
- "You do not have to be strong right now."
- "I am here, and I am not leaving."

Notice how it feels to say those words. Notice if there is resistance, emotion, or relief. Whatever comes up is okay.

This exercise can be repeated as many times as you need. Some people feel nothing the first time. Some people feel everything. Both are normal.

## Re-Parenting Yourself

Re-parenting is the practice of giving yourself, as an adult, what you needed as a child but did not receive. It is not about blaming your parents. It is about recognizing what was missing and filling that gap yourself.

Here is what re-parenting looks like in practice:

### If You Needed Safety

Create structure and consistency in your daily life, even in here. Same wake-up time. Same routine. Predictability is safety for your nervous system. When you feel unsafe, place your hand on your chest and say: "I am safe right now in this moment."

### If You Needed Validation

Start speaking to yourself the way a good parent would. When you accomplish something, acknowledge it: "That was hard, and I did it." When you make a mistake, respond with guidance instead of cruelty: "That did not work. Here is what I can try next."

### If You Needed Protection

Learn to set boundaries. You have the right to say no. You have the right to walk away from conversations that harm you. You have the right to protect your peace. The child inside you is watching how you treat yourself now — show them that you will fight for your own well-being.

### If You Needed Affection

This is hard in an environment that punishes tenderness. But you can still practice self-compassion. Write yourself a kind letter. Treat your body with care — stretch, drink water, take care of your hygiene not because you have to but because you are worth taking care of. Let yourself enjoy small things without guilt.

### If You Needed to Be Seen

Start seeing yourself. Keep a journal. Record your thoughts, your growth, your struggles. Bear witness to your own life. You matter whether or not anyone else notices.

## The Inner Child and Forgiveness

One of the most powerful things you can do for your inner child is to separate them from your adult actions. The child who was hurt is not the same as the adult who hurt others. Both are real. Both are you. But the child deserves compassion regardless of what the adult did.

This is not about excusing harm. It is about understanding that healing the child inside you is one of the most important steps toward making sure the adult you are becoming does not repeat the same patterns.

## Journal Prompts

1. Which of the five core wounds do you carry? Write about a specific childhood memory where you first felt that wound. What did you need in that moment that you did not receive?

2. If you could go back and say one thing to yourself as a child — at the age when things were hardest — what would you say? Write it as a letter.

3. What is one way you can practice re-parenting yourself this week? Be specific — not "be kinder to myself" but a concrete action you can take within your current circumstances.
