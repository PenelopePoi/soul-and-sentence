# Section 22: Job Readiness & Career Planning

Fair chance employers, Ban the Box laws, resume building, interview strategies.

## The Reality

Finding a job with a criminal record is harder than it should be. That is the truth. Studies show that a criminal record reduces callback rates by roughly 50%. For Black and brown applicants with records, the impact is even greater.

But harder does not mean impossible. Millions of people with criminal records are employed right now — in skilled trades, in offices, in warehouses, in their own businesses. The landscape is changing. More employers are recognizing that a person's worst moment does not define their potential. Laws are shifting in your favor. Industries are desperate for workers.

This section gives you the practical tools you need to find, apply for, and land a job — honestly and strategically.

## Ban the Box Laws

"Ban the Box" refers to laws and policies that remove the conviction history checkbox from job applications. The idea is that you should be evaluated on your qualifications first, and your record discussed later — not used to screen you out before anyone reads your resume.

### What Ban the Box Does

- Removes the question "Have you ever been convicted of a crime?" from the initial application.
- Delays the background check until later in the hiring process (usually after an interview or conditional offer).
- Gives you the chance to be seen as a person before being seen as a record.

### What Ban the Box Does NOT Do

- It does not make your record invisible. Employers can still ask about your record later in the process.
- It does not apply to all employers. In most states, it covers government jobs and sometimes large private employers, but not all businesses.
- It does not guarantee you will be hired. It guarantees you will not be automatically rejected at the application stage.

### Where It Applies

As of 2026, over 37 states and 150 cities/counties have some form of Ban the Box law. Florida has Ban the Box for state government jobs. Check your specific county and city for additional protections.

To find out what applies where you are going: search "[your city/county] fair chance hiring" or contact a local reentry organization.

## Fair Chance Employers

These are companies that have publicly committed to hiring people with criminal records. Many are major national companies:

### Large Employers Known for Fair Chance Hiring

- **Amazon** — Warehouse and delivery positions
- **Walmart** — Retail and warehouse (conducts background checks but considers them individually)
- **Target** — Banned the Box, considers applicants individually
- **Home Depot** — Evaluates on a case-by-case basis
- **McDonald's / Burger King / Wendy's** — Many franchise locations hire with records
- **FedEx / UPS** — Warehouse and sorting positions
- **Greyston Bakery** — Open hiring, no questions asked
- **Dave's Killer Bread** — Founded by someone with a record, actively recruits
- **Goodwill** — Known for second-chance hiring
- **Koch Industries (Georgia-Pacific, Molex)** — Major fair chance employer
- **JPMorgan Chase** — Committed to fair chance hiring for many positions
- **Starbucks** — Partners with fair chance organizations

### Trades and Industries That Are More Open

Some industries care more about what you can do than what you did:

- **Construction** — Especially if you have skills or certifications
- **Welding** — High demand, well-paying, and generally record-friendly
- **Trucking (CDL)** — Some restrictions apply depending on the offense, but many companies hire with records
- **Warehouse / Logistics** — High turnover means constant hiring
- **Landscaping / Lawn Care** — Many small companies hire without background checks
- **Food Service / Restaurants** — Fast-paced hiring, flexible on records
- **Moving companies** — Physical labor, often hiring
- **Auto repair** — Skill-based, many independent shops hire with records
- **Janitorial / Cleaning services** — Commercial cleaning companies frequently hire

### Resources for Finding Fair Chance Employers

- **70 Million Jobs** (www.70millionjobs.com) — Job board specifically for people with records
- **Honest Jobs** (www.honestjobs.co) — Fair chance employer job board
- **National HIRE Network** (www.hirenetwork.org) — Resources and employer directory
- **Reentry organizations in your area** — Many maintain lists of local employers who hire

## Building a Resume with Gaps

A resume with a gap in employment is not unusual — millions of people have them (caregivers, people who were ill, career changers). Here is how to handle yours.

### Option 1: Functional Resume (Skills-Based)

Instead of listing jobs chronologically, organize your resume by skills. This puts what you can do front and center.

**Example Structure:**

> **[Your Name]**
> [Phone Number] | [Email Address] | [City, State]
>
> **Skills Summary**
> - 5+ years experience in warehouse operations, including inventory management, forklift operation, and shipping/receiving
> - Strong work ethic, reliable, and detail-oriented
> - Certified in [any certifications you have]
>
> **Relevant Skills & Experience**
> - Inventory management and organization
> - Equipment operation (forklift, pallet jack)
> - Team leadership and training
> - Customer service and communication
> - Physical stamina and endurance
>
> **Work History**
> [Job Title] — [Company], [City, State] — [Dates]
> [Job Title] — [Company], [City, State] — [Dates]
>
> **Education & Training**
> [GED, degree, certifications, or programs completed — including programs completed during incarceration]

### Option 2: Include Incarceration Productively

If you completed significant programs, earned certifications, or developed skills during your incarceration, you can include that time productively:

> **Professional Development** — [Years]
> - Completed [specific program or certification]
> - Served as [role, if applicable — tutor, mentor, crew lead]
> - Earned [GED, vocational certificate, etc.]

This fills the gap honestly without making the resume about your conviction.

### Resume Tips

- **Keep it to one page.** Employers spend an average of 7 seconds scanning a resume.
- **Use action verbs:** Managed, operated, trained, organized, maintained, built, repaired.
- **Include any certifications:** OSHA, forklift, food handler, CDL, CPR, welding — anything.
- **Do not lie.** Do not fabricate employment. Background checks will reveal it, and you will lose the opportunity and your credibility.
- **Have someone proofread it.** Ask a case manager, reentry counselor, or trusted person to review it.

## The Interview: Addressing Your Record

This is the part most people dread. Here is the truth: most employers who invite you to an interview already know you may have a record (especially if you applied through a fair chance program). The interview is your chance to show them who you are now.

### When to Bring It Up

- **If they ask:** Answer honestly. Do not volunteer information they did not ask for.
- **If they do not ask:** You are not obligated to bring it up. However, if a background check is coming, it is often better to address it briefly during the interview than to let them discover it on their own.

### The Interview Script

Use this framework. Practice it out loud until it feels natural:

**Step 1: Acknowledge it briefly.**
"I want to be upfront with you. I do have a criminal record."

**Step 2: Take responsibility without over-explaining.**
"I made mistakes in my past that I take full responsibility for. I am not here to make excuses."

**Step 3: Show what has changed.**
"During that time, I [completed a program, earned a certification, worked on myself, developed new skills]. I have used that experience to become a different person."

**Step 4: Redirect to the future.**
"What I am focused on now is building a stable life, being reliable, and proving myself through my work. I am looking for an opportunity to do that, and I believe I can be a strong asset to your team."

**Step 5: Stop talking.**
Do not over-explain. Do not apologize repeatedly. State your case, then let them respond.

### Sample Script (Tailored)

> "I want to be honest with you — I do have a record. About [X years] ago, I was convicted of [general category, not graphic details]. I take full responsibility for that. While I was incarcerated, I earned my [GED/certification], completed [program], and spent a lot of time working on becoming a better person. I am looking for an opportunity to prove myself through hard work and reliability. I am ready to be a dependable member of your team."

### What NOT to Do in an Interview

- Do not lie about your record. If caught, you will be terminated and may face legal consequences.
- Do not over-share details of your offense. Keep it general: "I was convicted of a felony" is enough unless they ask for more.
- Do not bad-mouth the system, the judge, or anyone else. Take ownership.
- Do not act like they owe you a chance. Show them why you are worth one.

## High-Demand Trades and How to Get Into Them

If you can learn a skilled trade, your employment options expand dramatically. Many trades offer strong wages, job security, and do not require a four-year degree.

| Trade | Avg. Starting Pay | Training Path | Record-Friendly? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Welding | $18-25/hr | Vocational program or apprenticeship | Generally yes |
| Electrical | $17-22/hr | Apprenticeship (4-5 years) | Varies by state |
| Plumbing | $16-22/hr | Apprenticeship (4-5 years) | Generally yes |
| HVAC | $17-24/hr | Vocational program or apprenticeship | Generally yes |
| CDL Truck Driving | $20-28/hr | CDL school (4-8 weeks) | Depends on offense |
| Carpentry | $16-22/hr | Apprenticeship or on-the-job | Generally yes |
| Auto Mechanic | $15-22/hr | Vocational program | Generally yes |
| CNC Machining | $18-26/hr | Vocational program | Generally yes |

### How to Access Training

- **Vocational programs in your facility:** Ask about available programs. Complete anything offered.
- **Community colleges:** Many offer trade programs with financial aid. Apply for Pell Grants (incarcerated individuals became eligible again in 2023).
- **Apprenticeship programs:** Earn while you learn. Search at www.apprenticeship.gov.
- **Reentry workforce programs:** Many cities have programs specifically for people with records. Ask your case manager or search "[your city] reentry workforce program."
- **Union halls:** Some unions actively recruit from reentry populations. IBEW (electrical), UA (plumbing/pipefitting), and LIUNA (laborers) are worth investigating.

## Self-Employment

If traditional employment proves too difficult, self-employment is a real option. Many trades and services lend themselves to independent work:

- Lawn care and landscaping
- Pressure washing
- Moving and hauling
- Handyman services
- Auto detailing
- Cleaning services (residential and commercial)

Starting costs can be minimal. A lawn care business can start with a used mower and a truck. A pressure washing business can start with a rented machine and flyers.

Research local business license requirements. Some states restrict certain licenses for people with felonies, but many do not. A reentry organization or local Small Business Development Center (SBDC) can help you navigate this.

## Job Readiness Checklist

- [ ] Updated resume (one page, skills-based)
- [ ] Valid state ID or driver's license
- [ ] Social Security card
- [ ] Practice your interview script about your record (say it out loud 10 times)
- [ ] Research fair chance employers in your area
- [ ] Apply for at least 5 jobs per week
- [ ] Contact local reentry workforce program
- [ ] Research trade programs and apprenticeships
- [ ] Prepare references (3 people who can speak to your character and work ethic)
- [ ] Set up a professional voicemail on your phone
- [ ] Create a free email address for job applications (professional — your name, not a nickname)

## Journal Prompts

1. What skills do you have right now — from any source, including work before incarceration, programs inside, or natural abilities? List everything, no matter how small it seems. Now look at that list. Which of those skills could translate into employment or self-employment?

2. Write out and practice your interview script for addressing your record. Read it out loud. Does it feel honest? Does it feel like you — not like a performance? Revise it until it does.

3. If you could have any job or career in five years, what would it be? What steps would you need to take to get there? Be specific — not "I want a good job" but the actual job, the training required, and the first step you can take.
