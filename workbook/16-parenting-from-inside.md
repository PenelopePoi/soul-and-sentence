# Section 16: Parenting from the Inside

Being separated from your children is one of the deepest pains. But absence does not have to mean disconnection.

## The Weight of It

There is no way to sugarcoat this. Being locked up while your children grow up is one of the hardest things a human being can endure. You miss birthdays. You miss first days of school. You miss the moments when they needed you and you were not there.

The guilt can be crushing. And if you are not careful, the guilt will convince you that you have nothing to offer — that your children are better off without you, that you have already failed too badly to recover.

That is not true.

Research consistently shows that children who maintain a relationship with an incarcerated parent — even through letters, phone calls, and visits — do better emotionally, academically, and socially than children who lose that connection entirely. Your presence matters, even from in here. Your effort matters. Your love matters.

This section is about how to be a parent from the inside — not a perfect parent, but a present one.

## What Your Children Need to Hear

Children of incarcerated parents carry specific fears and wounds. Whether they are toddlers, school-age, or teenagers, there are things they need to hear from you — repeatedly, consistently, and sincerely.

### "This is not your fault."

Children, especially young children, are egocentric by nature. They think the world revolves around them — which also means they think everything is their fault. Many children of incarcerated parents secretly believe that something they did caused your absence. They need to hear, clearly and more than once, that your situation is not because of anything they did.

### "I love you and that will never change."

Your child may be angry at you. They may refuse your calls or not want to visit. That does not mean they do not need to hear that you love them. Anger and love coexist, especially in children. Keep saying it. Even when it feels like they are not listening.

### "It is okay to feel how you feel."

Give your child permission to be angry, sad, confused, or embarrassed. Do not ask them to pretend everything is fine. Do not guilt them for being upset. Say: "You have every right to feel that way. I understand."

### "I am working on being better."

Children do not need you to be perfect. They need to know you are trying. Tell them what you are doing — "I am in a class about being a better parent" or "I am reading a book about how to handle my anger" or "I am working on myself so I can be the parent you deserve." Be specific. Let them see that growth is real.

### "You are going to be okay."

Children need reassurance about their own future. They need to know that your situation does not define their destiny. You can tell them: "What happened to me does not have to happen to you. You have choices. And I will support you no matter what."

## Staying Connected Through Letters

Letters are one of the most powerful tools you have. A phone call lasts a few minutes and then it is gone. A letter can be read over and over. Your child can hold it. They can keep it under their pillow. They can read it when they miss you at 2 AM and nobody else is awake.

### Tips for Writing to Your Children

**Be age-appropriate.** A letter to a 5-year-old looks different from a letter to a 15-year-old. For young children, draw pictures, use simple words, ask about their favorite things. For older children, be more honest about your feelings and ask about theirs.

**Be consistent.** A letter every week or every two weeks means more than one long letter followed by months of silence. Consistency builds trust. Even if you can only write a few lines, send something.

**Ask questions.** Show genuine interest in their lives. "What are you learning in school?" "Do you still like drawing?" "Who is your best friend right now?" "What is the funniest thing that happened this week?" When they know you care about the details, they feel seen.

**Share your world (carefully).** You do not need to describe the hard parts. But you can say, "I saw a bird outside my window today and thought of you" or "I have been reading a book about space and I know you would love it." Let them know you are a real person living a real life, not just a voice on the phone or a name on an envelope.

**Apologize without burdening.** If you have things to apologize for, do it simply: "I am sorry I am not there. You deserved better, and I am working to make things right." Do not make the letter about your guilt. Make it about them.

**Include things they can do.** Send a crossword puzzle. Write a story and ask them to write the ending. Draw a picture and ask them to color it. Create something interactive that makes the letter feel like time spent together.

### Sample Letter Framework (Younger Children)

> Dear [Name],
>
> I think about you every single day. I want you to know that I love you so much and nothing will ever change that.
>
> I have a question for you: What is your favorite thing to do after school right now? I really want to know!
>
> I drew you a picture of [something]. I hope it makes you smile.
>
> You are so special and so loved. Never forget that.
>
> Love always,
> [Your name]

### Sample Letter Framework (Older Children / Teens)

> Dear [Name],
>
> I know things are not the way either of us wants them to be. I think about that every day, and I want you to know that I take responsibility for my part in it.
>
> I have been doing a lot of thinking and working on myself in here. I am learning about [what you are working on]. I am not the same person I was, and I am going to keep growing.
>
> How are you doing? I mean really. You can be honest with me — even if you are angry, even if you are hurt. I can handle it. I would rather know the truth than have you pretend everything is fine.
>
> You are the most important person in my world, and I am proud of who you are becoming.
>
> Love,
> [Your name]

## Visits: Making Them Count

If you are able to have visits with your children, the quality of that time matters more than the length.

**Do not spend the entire visit making promises about the future.** Be present. Play games. Ask about their lives. Let them talk. Let there be comfortable silence. They need to feel your presence, not hear a speech.

**Do not interrogate them.** "Are you being good? Are your grades up? Are you staying out of trouble?" feels like surveillance, not love. Ask open questions. Listen more than you talk.

**Handle goodbyes carefully.** Goodbyes are the hardest part. Do not fall apart in front of them — it terrifies children. Be warm, be steady, and give them something to look forward to: "I will write you a letter this week" or "Next time you come, let us play that game again."

**If they are upset, do not take it personally.** A child who is angry during a visit is not rejecting you. They are showing you how much your absence hurts. Receive it. Do not punish them for it.

## Breaking the Cycle

If you are in here partly because of patterns you inherited — if your parent was incarcerated, if there was abuse or addiction in your family, if you grew up in the same system — then you know something about cycles.

Breaking the cycle does not mean being a perfect parent. It means being a conscious one. It means looking at the patterns you inherited and deciding, one choice at a time, not to pass them on.

Here are some questions to consider:

- What did I need from my parents that I did not receive?
- Am I repeating that same absence or harm with my own children?
- What is one specific thing I can do differently, even from in here?
- What do I want my child to say about me in 20 years?

The cycle does not break all at once. It breaks in small moments — in the letter you write instead of giving up, in the apology you offer instead of defending yourself, in the consistency you show even when it is hard.

Your children do not need a perfect parent. They need one who tries. They need one who shows up, even from behind these walls.

## Resources for Incarcerated Parents

- **Angel Tree (Prison Fellowship):** Provides Christmas gifts and summer camp for children of incarcerated parents. www.prisonfellowship.org
- **Sesame Street — Little Children, Big Challenges: Incarceration:** Free toolkit designed to help children cope. www.sesamestreet.org/incarceration
- **National Resource Center on Children and Families of the Incarcerated:** nrccfi.camden.rutgers.edu
- **Storybook Project:** Programs that allow incarcerated parents to record themselves reading books aloud, which are then sent to their children. Ask your facility if a local program is available.

## Journal Prompts

1. Write a letter to your child (or a child you care about) that you may or may not send. Tell them what you wish they understood about your situation, and tell them what you see in them that makes you proud.

2. What patterns from your own childhood are you most afraid of repeating? What is one concrete thing you are doing or could do to break that cycle?

3. Imagine your child as an adult, looking back on this time. What do you want them to remember about how you handled your absence? What story do you want them to be able to tell?
