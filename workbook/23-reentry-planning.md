# Section 23: Reentry Planning & Transition Preparation

Reentry begins long before the gate opens. Plan months or years in advance.

## Why Reentry Planning Matters

The first 72 hours after release are the most dangerous. The first 30 days are when most people fail. Not because they are weak, but because they were not prepared.

Imagine being dropped into a city with no phone, no car, limited money, no job lined up, and nowhere to live — while simultaneously dealing with the emotional overload of freedom after confinement. Without a plan, the old patterns are right there waiting.

Reentry planning is not something you do the week before you get out. It is something you start now — no matter how far away your release date is. Every checklist item you complete while inside is one less crisis you face on the outside.

## Before Release Checklist

These are things you should try to accomplish while you are still incarcerated. Start as early as possible.

### Identification and Documents

- [ ] **State ID or Driver's License** — Many states allow you to apply before release. Ask your case manager. Without ID, you cannot get a job, open a bank account, or access most services. This is your number one priority.
- [ ] **Social Security Card** — You can request a replacement card by mail. Form SS-5 is available at www.ssa.gov or through your facility's law library.
- [ ] **Birth Certificate** — Contact the vital records office in the state where you were born. Costs vary ($10-30). Some reentry organizations will help cover this cost.
- [ ] **DD-214 (if you are a veteran)** — Contact the National Personnel Records Center. Veterans have access to additional reentry benefits.
- [ ] **Any certifications earned during incarceration** — GED, vocational certificates, program completion documents. Keep copies of everything.

### Housing

- [ ] **Identify where you will live on day one.** Family member, friend, halfway house, transitional housing, or shelter. Have a confirmed address.
- [ ] **Apply for transitional housing if needed.** Waiting lists can be months long. Apply early.
- [ ] **Research housing restrictions.** Some public housing has restrictions for certain offenses. Know what applies to you.
- [ ] **Have a backup plan.** If Plan A falls through, where will you go? Have Plans B and C.

### Employment

- [ ] **Build your resume while inside.** Use the skills-based format from Section 22.
- [ ] **Complete any available vocational programs.** Any certification helps.
- [ ] **Research fair chance employers** in the city where you will be released.
- [ ] **Contact reentry workforce programs** in your release area. Many require enrollment before release.

### Healthcare

- [ ] **Apply for Medicaid.** In most states, you can apply 30-90 days before release. Medicaid covers medical, mental health, and substance abuse treatment.
- [ ] **Request your medical records** from the facility. You are entitled to them.
- [ ] **If you take medication,** ensure you have at least a 30-day supply upon release. Many facilities provide a limited supply. Know what medications you take and their dosages.
- [ ] **Identify a primary care doctor or clinic** near where you will be living. Community health centers offer sliding-scale fees.

### Financial

- [ ] **Open a bank account** (if possible before release — some programs arrange this).
- [ ] **Know how much money you will have upon release.** Gate money, savings from prison work, any funds being held for you.
- [ ] **Create a budget** for your first 30 days. See Section 21.
- [ ] **Apply for benefits you are eligible for:** SNAP (food assistance), Medicaid, state assistance programs.

### Legal

- [ ] **Understand your supervision conditions.** Probation or parole requirements, check-in schedules, restricted areas, curfews. Know them before you leave.
- [ ] **Know your probation/parole officer's name and contact information.**
- [ ] **Understand any fees or fines** you owe and the payment schedule.
- [ ] **Research your rights regarding record sealing or expungement** (see state-specific guidelines).

### Personal Support

- [ ] **Identify at least three people** you can call in a crisis.
- [ ] **Identify at least one person** who will support your recovery/growth, not pull you backward.
- [ ] **If applicable, connect with a faith community** in your release area before you leave.
- [ ] **If applicable, locate NA/AA meetings** near your release location.

## First 30 Days Checklist

The first month is about stabilization. You are not building a career yet. You are establishing a foundation.

### Week 1: Survive and Settle

- [ ] Report to probation/parole officer within the required timeframe.
- [ ] Confirm your housing situation. If your plan fell through, contact a shelter or reentry organization immediately.
- [ ] Get your state ID or driver's license if you do not already have one.
- [ ] Apply for SNAP (food stamps) if eligible. Many states allow same-day enrollment for people just released.
- [ ] Open a bank account.
- [ ] Get a phone. Prepaid phones are fine. You need a number for employers, your PO, and your support system.
- [ ] Eat regular meals. Sleep on a schedule. Your body needs to recalibrate.

### Week 2: Build Routine

- [ ] Begin applying for jobs (at least 5 applications per week).
- [ ] If enrolled in a reentry program, attend every session.
- [ ] If in recovery, attend your first meeting. Get a sponsor if the program offers one.
- [ ] Set up a daily routine: wake time, meals, job search time, personal time, sleep time.
- [ ] Reconnect with supportive family or friends — but set boundaries. Do not let guilt or obligation drag you into unhealthy situations.
- [ ] Avoid old neighborhoods, old associates, and old patterns. This is the hardest thing on this list, and the most important.

### Week 3: Gain Momentum

- [ ] Follow up on job applications. Call or visit employers who have not responded.
- [ ] If you have not found work yet, expand your search. Consider temp agencies — they often hire quickly and with fewer restrictions.
- [ ] Enroll in any available training or education programs.
- [ ] Check your finances. Are you on budget? Adjust if needed.
- [ ] Keep all appointments — PO meetings, medical appointments, program sessions. Consistency builds trust.

### Week 4: Assess and Adjust

- [ ] Take stock of where you are. What is working? What is not?
- [ ] If housing is unstable, seek assistance now. Do not wait for a crisis.
- [ ] If employment is not happening, seek help from a reentry workforce specialist.
- [ ] Celebrate what you have accomplished. You survived the hardest month.
- [ ] Write down your goals for the next 60 days.

## First 90 Days Checklist

By month three, you should be moving from survival to stability.

- [ ] Steady employment or a clear path to it.
- [ ] Stable housing (not couch-surfing or in a shelter if possible).
- [ ] Active compliance with all probation/parole requirements.
- [ ] If in recovery, consistent attendance at meetings or treatment.
- [ ] A functioning budget that you are following.
- [ ] Emergency fund started (even $50 is a start).
- [ ] Medical care established (primary care doctor, mental health provider if needed).
- [ ] A daily routine that supports your goals.
- [ ] At least one supportive relationship that you are investing in.
- [ ] Research into next steps: vocational training, education, record sealing/expungement, or career advancement.

## Housing Resources

Housing is often the biggest challenge upon release. Here are resources to explore:

### Types of Post-Release Housing

**Halfway Houses / Residential Reentry Centers:** Structured environments with rules, curfews, and support services. Often required by the Bureau of Prisons or as a condition of release. Stay is typically 3-6 months.

**Transitional Housing:** Less structured than halfway houses, typically 6-24 months. Often run by nonprofits. May include case management, job assistance, and life skills training.

**Sober Living Houses:** If you are in recovery, these provide a substance-free living environment with peer accountability. Costs vary but are often more affordable than market-rate apartments.

**Public Housing:** Available through local Public Housing Authorities (PHA). Some restrictions apply for certain offenses (drug-related offenses have specific federal rules, and violent/sex offenses may be excluded). However, many offenses do NOT disqualify you — ask before assuming you are ineligible.

**Supportive Housing:** Combines housing with services (mental health, substance abuse treatment, case management). Often for people experiencing homelessness or with significant challenges.

### National Housing Resources

- **National Reentry Resource Center:** www.nationalreentryresourcecenter.org
- **HUD (Housing and Urban Development):** www.hud.gov — Search for local housing authorities
- **Salvation Army:** Often provides emergency shelter and transitional housing. www.salvationarmy.org
- **Oxford Houses (Sober Living):** www.oxfordhouse.org — Self-supporting recovery houses in 44 states
- **211:** Dial 2-1-1 from any phone for free referrals to local housing, food, and social services
- **Catholic Charities:** www.catholiccharitiesusa.org — Housing assistance regardless of faith

### Tips for Finding Housing with a Record

- Apply to multiple places simultaneously. Rejection is common — do not let one "no" stop you.
- Be upfront about your record when required. Lying on a housing application can result in eviction.
- Get letters of recommendation from program staff, case managers, or employers.
- Consider roommate situations to reduce costs and increase your options.
- If denied housing, ask why. Some denials can be appealed, especially if the landlord did not follow fair housing laws.

## The Emotional Side of Reentry

Nobody talks enough about this: reentry is emotionally overwhelming. Even when things go well, the adjustment is intense.

You may experience:

- **Sensory overload.** The noise, the options, the movement of the outside world can be disorienting.
- **Guilt.** About what you put your family through. About being free while people you know are still inside.
- **Anxiety.** About failing, about going back, about whether you can actually do this.
- **Grief.** For the time you lost. For the person you were before. For the relationships that did not survive.
- **Anger.** At the system, at yourself, at the gap between expectations and reality.
- **Loneliness.** Even surrounded by people, reentry can be isolating. Nobody fully understands what you went through.

These feelings are normal. They are not signs that you are failing. They are signs that you are human.

Find at least one person you can be honest with about how you are feeling. A counselor, a mentor, a sponsor, a friend who will listen without judging. You do not have to do this alone.

## Journal Prompts

1. Where are you in your reentry planning right now? Go through the "Before Release" checklist and be honest about what you have done and what you still need to do. What is your next step?

2. What is your biggest fear about reentry? Write about it in detail. Then write about what you will do if that fear comes true. Having a plan for the worst case takes away some of its power.

3. Imagine yourself 90 days after release. Where are you living? What are you doing for work? Who is in your life? What does a typical Tuesday look like? Describe it in detail — make it real enough to work toward.
